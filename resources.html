Students

I am an engineer trained as an economist. I work with undergraduate, master's and Ph.D. students on environmental and development economics. My work covers land rights, water and climate, and uses data from satellites, maps and surveys.

Working with me

Undergraduates

Who

Current IU students in economics, policy, environmental science, engineering or data science.

What you might do

Help on research projects: preparing data, making maps and tables, and reading past studies. This can lead to an honors thesis.

How to start

Email me or come to office hours. See also the O'Neill Honors Program.

Master's students

Who

Students in O'Neill master's programs and those who plan to apply.

What you might do

How to start

Apply through O'Neill: degrees and certificates · how to apply. Current students can email me.

Ph.D. students

Planning your path

Undergraduates who want to do research

  • Courses: calculus, linear algebra, statistics, econometrics and one programming course. If you plan a Ph.D. in economics, take real analysis too.
  • Research experience: working on a research project or an honors thesis counts more than one extra class.
  • After graduation: many students work for two years as a research assistant before a Ph.D. See PREDOC job listings, NBER RA listings, and J-PAL's advice for landing an RA job.
  • Is a Ph.D. right for you? J-PAL's guide to Ph.D. admissions, Adam Guren's application advice, and Northwestern on whether you need this job first.
  • Programs: the AEA Summer Training Program and AEA resources for students.

Graduate students

  • From classes to a paper: in your first year, keep a list of questions and datasets. By your second year, have one project with data in hand.
  • Keep your work in order: write code that others can check. Journals now ask for it (AEA data and code policy). Tools are in step 6 below.
  • Present early: at student seminars and at meetings such as those of the Association of Environmental and Resource Economists.
  • Job market: jobs are posted on JOE and EconJobMarket. Jobs in policy and research groups are good choices too.
  • More advice: a guide to economics graduate school, written by many students.

Getting started in research

Each step shows where undergraduates and graduate students should start.

1. Start with a question

Undergraduates: pick one recent paper you like and ask what it does not answer. Graduate students: write a one-page plan we can talk about.

Good projects start from a policy-relevant question you care about, not from a method. Read recent papers in the area, note what they leave open, and ask what data would let you answer it. When you are ready, write a one-page note: the question, why it matters, the data, and how you would identify the effect. Overleaf LaTeX templates are a good place to write it.

2. Learn causal inference

Undergraduates: start with The Remix and Econometrics with R. Graduate students: add the Chernozhukov book, the Ph.D. course and the difference-in-differences links.

  • Causal Inference: The Remix (Cunningham, free online): the new second edition of The Mixtape, with more on difference-in-differences and synthetic control (start here)
  • Applied Causal Inference Powered by ML and AI (Chernozhukov, Hansen, Kallus, Spindler and Syrgkanis, free online): double machine learning and modern causal methods, with R and Python code
  • Introduction to Econometrics with R
  • Applied Empirical Methods (Ph.D. course)
  • Difference-in-differences resources (Pedro H. C. Sant'Anna)
  • Difference-in-differences course (Clément de Chaisemartin)
  • Staggered DiD software packages (Asjad Naqvi)
  • Difference-in-differences reading group
  • Online Causal Inference Seminar
  • Discrete Choice Methods with Simulation (for valuation and choice models)
3. Build your coding skills

Undergraduates: learn one language well (Stata, R or Python). Graduate students: learn a second one and write code that someone else can run.

Stata

  • Stata guides (University of Wisconsin–Madison)
  • Stata tutorials (Princeton University)
  • reghdfe: fast fixed-effects OLS and ppmlhdfe: fast fixed-effects Poisson
  • gtools: big data processing
  • Top 25 Stata visualizations with code

Python, R and machine learning

  • Stata to Python equivalents
  • Library of Statistical Techniques (LOST): the same method in Stata, R and Python
  • DoWhy and EconML (Python)
  • Economics and data science
  • Machine learning in economics
  • Data Science for Public Policy (Chen, Rubin and Cornwall, 2021): R, prediction and machine learning applied to policy questions
  • Knowledge-Guided Machine Learning (Karpatne, Kannan and Kumar, 2022): building scientific knowledge into ML models
4. Work with spatial, satellite and survey data

Undergraduates: make one map in QGIS from free data. Graduate students: turn satellite data into a yearly dataset in Earth Engine, R or Python.

Most of my projects combine satellite and map data with government records or surveys. All the tools and data below are free.

Learn the tools

  • QGIS (open-source GIS) and its training manual
  • Google Earth Engine guides and the open book Cloud-Based Remote Sensing with Google Earth Engine
  • Geocomputation with R and Geographic Data Science with Python (free online books)
  • NASA ARSET: free remote sensing trainings for applied users
  • Spatial Thoughts blog (Ujaval Gandhi): GIS and Google Earth Engine tutorials

Open satellite and geospatial data

  • Earth Engine Data Catalog and Microsoft Planetary Computer: analysis-ready satellite and climate data
  • USGS EarthExplorer (Landsat) and Copernicus Data Space (Sentinel)
  • Global Forest Change (Hansen et al.) and MapBiomas: forest loss and land cover
  • CHIRPS rainfall and Copernicus Climate Data Store (ERA5 weather)
  • VIIRS night lights and NASA SEDAC (population and socioeconomic layers)
  • geoBoundaries: administrative boundaries
  • AidData GeoQuery: subnational data, ready to download

Satellite data in economics

  • The View from Above: Applications of Satellite Data in Economics (Donaldson and Storeygard, 2016)
  • The Benefits and Pitfalls of Using Satellite Data for Causal Inference (Jain, 2020)

Survey data

  • Automatic download and processing of DHS surveys
5. Learn the physical side

Undergraduates: read one of the articles below. Graduate students: learn how your weather, water or land data are made before you use them.

I came to economics from engineering. Good environmental economics needs to understand the physical world: how water moves, how weather data are made, and what a satellite can and cannot see.

  • Using Weather Data and Climate Model Output in Economic Analyses of Climate Change (Auffhammer, Hsiang, Schlenker and Sobel, 2013)
  • What Do We Learn from the Weather? The New Climate-Economy Literature (Dell, Jones and Olken, 2014)
  • Climate Econometrics (Hsiang, 2016)
  • USGS Water Science School: groundwater, surface water and the water cycle
  • NASA GRACE (groundwater from gravity) and TerraClimate (monthly water balance)
6. Write, share and reproduce

Undergraduates: learn LaTeX and keep your code in one folder with a short README file. Graduate students: use Git, and make sure every result can be rebuilt from the raw data.

  • Overleaf: learn LaTeX and templates
  • Code and Data for the Social Sciences (Gentzkow and Shapiro)
  • The Missing Semester of Your CS Education (MIT): command line, Git and tools engineers use daily
  • Happy Git and GitHub for the useR
